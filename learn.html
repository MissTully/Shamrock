3Women in Kilts: A Guide for the Ladies👑 Krewe style · wear it your wayClick to learn ▾☘

What you'll learn

  • Yes, women wear kilts, and in the Krewe of Shamrock they do so proudly. Women across Ireland, Scotland, and the diaspora have worn kilts and kilted skirts for well over a century.
  • Know your three terms: a true kilt is one wrapped length of pleated wool with straps and buckles; a kilted skirt is built the same way but lighter, closer-fitting, and offered in mini, knee, midi, and ankle lengths; a tartan skirt is any skirt sewn from tartan cloth. All three are welcome on the route.
  • Choosing your cloth: an Irish county or family tartan, the green-gold-and-white Irish National tartan, traditional solid saffron, a Scottish clan tartan, or a universal tartan like Black Watch that anyone may wear.
  • The signature parade look for many Krewe women is a corset or bodice over a white Celtic blouse; a Krewe t-shirt is always welcome too. A tartan sash pinned at the shoulder with a brooch is the classic women's alternative to a man's fly plaid.
  • Practical wisdom: the parade route is about four miles, so comfortable shoes win; kilts sit at the natural waist, so measure before ordering; and custom kilts from Scotland or Ireland can take six to twelve weeks, so order early.

Krewe Style

Kilts in the Krewe

What we wear on parade day
☘ Click to read the story ▾

In the Krewe of Shamrock, the kilt is the uniform of the day, and both great Gaelic traditions are welcome on the route. Some members march Irish, in saffron or solid-color kilts and county tartans. Others march Scottish, in clan tartans handed down the family line. Wear whichever tradition calls to you; the only requirement is that you wear it proudly.

On parade morning you'll see every version of it rolling out together: saffron beside clan plaid, county tartan beside solid green, and ghillie shirts everywhere in the Florida sun. Add a sporran, kilt hose, and a good handful of beads, and you're parade-ready. New to kilts? The lesson cards above sort the styles, and any member will happily talk tartan with you.

Heritage & Style

The Colors of the Cloth

What the hues of a tartan mean
☘ Click to read the story ▾

Every color woven into a kilt carries a story. Green speaks of Ireland's lush scenery and of patriotism, tying the wearer to the land itself and the spirit of Saint Patrick. Blue recalls the ancient Irish aristocracy and loyalty; long before green took over, blue was the color of Gaelic kings. Gold stands for warmth and prosperity, the richness of the culture and the success of the Irish abroad.

Red honors courage and tenacity, the sacrifices made through every hard chapter of Irish history. Black represents power and persistence, the enduring strength of a people who would not be worn down. And saffron, that warm golden-orange of the traditional Irish kilt, is the banner of the Gaelic revival: a color chosen deliberately to say we are Irish, and proudly so.

So when the Krewe rolls out on parade morning in green and gold, saffron and plaid, it isn't just a pretty picture. It's a whole history told in thread, and every member wearing it becomes part of the telling.

Kilt Myths & Legends

Sorting fact from folklore
☘ Click to read the story ▾

Few garments carry as many tall tales as the kilt. Did the Irish bring it to Scotland? Did tartan begin in the Highlands? Does the number of colors mark the wearer's rank, or hide a secret Ogham message woven into the cloth? The truth is often older, and stranger, than the legend.

The kilt-makers at Keltoi Gaelic Clothing have gathered the best-loved myths about kilts, tartan, and Highland wear and weighed each one against the historical record, from the Gaels of Dál Riata in their saffron léine and brat to the clan tartans devised in the weavers' sample books of the 1700s. A grand read for anyone proud of their Gaelic threads.

Did the Irish Invent the Kilt?

From the Celtic Fire blog
☘ Click to read the story ▾

Around 432 AD, in the days of Saint Patrick, what did the Irish actually wear? Author Erin O'Quinn paints the picture from old manuscripts, Roman accounts, Ireland's earliest legends, and garments pulled from the preserving depths of the bog: the linen léine belted at the waist, the woolen brat cloak, leather bróga, and the bright, status-marking colors a person was allowed to wear.

And the big question her headline asks: did the Irish Gaels, pushing into Scotland in the fifth century, hand the skirt-like léine to the Scots, or borrow it from the roaming Scoti? It's a debate that has "raged for over a hundred years," and her warm, witty post is a lovely corner of a blog full of all things Irish.

5The Inter-Krewe Council & Tampa's Krewes🤝 Parade culture · our wider familyClick to learn ▾☘

What you'll learn

  • A "krewe" is a social organization that parades and celebrates together; the word (an old-fashioned spelling of "crew") comes from the New Orleans Mardi Gras tradition of the 1850s.
  • Tampa Bay is home to dozens of krewes, and the Inter-Krewe Council (IKC) is the association that brings them together: coordinating the shared parade calendar, promoting goodwill between krewes, and championing charitable work across the bay.
  • Our "Big Four" parades each season are Children's Gasparilla, the Gasparilla Parade of Pirates, the Knights of Sant' Yago Knight Parade in historic Ybor City, and the Rough Riders St. Patrick's Day Parade.
  • Our own story began inside this krewe family: in 1998 our five founders walked the Ybor City parades as security for Ye Loyal Krewe of Grace O'Malley before petitioning to march as a krewe of their own in 1999.
  • Beyond the Big Four, the krewe also turns out for Santa Fest, Tampa Pride, and community events throughout the year.

Krewe History

From Grace O'Malley's Guard to a Krewe of Our Own

The founding story, 1998–1999
☘ Click to read the story ▾

Every krewe has an origin story, and ours begins on someone else's parade route. In 1998, five friends, Matt Glow, Tim Hubbell, Hayden Polk, Chris Jenkins, and Steve Hubbell, walked the Ybor City parades as security for Ye Loyal Krewe of Grace O'Malley. They kept the route safe, watched the krewe they guarded having the time of their lives, and came away with one shared thought: we should have a krewe of our own.

After the 1999 Gasparilla season they made it official. With Don Cassels serving as the group's first spokesperson, they petitioned the St. Patrick's Day Parade Committee, and in 1999 the Krewe of Shamrock marched for the first time, kilts on and bagpipes playing. From the very beginning the krewe was built as an Irish-themed social and charitable organization, and from the very beginning the motto has been the same: All for Fun, Fun for All.

Today the krewe continues to welcome new faces, and while we celebrate Irish and Scottish traditions with everything we've got, Irish or Scottish ancestry has never been required. What's required is much simpler: show up, pitch in, and bring the craic.

🛡 Parade Day Know-How

6Parade Day Rules & Safety🛡 Member know-how · required readingClick to learn ▾☘

What you'll learn

  • Boarding and leaving the float takes extreme caution: always step off backwards, and a designated safety person will be stationed there to assist you. Never stand on speaker or storage boxes.
  • Walkers stay to the sides or behind the float, never directly in front of it and never in the gap between the float and the tow vehicle. Krewe marshals and spotters walk the whole route to keep spacing safe.
  • Respect earned spots: don't move another member's bead bags or spot designations, including bags hanging from the hooks.
  • Drinks stay in opaque cups or tumblers; glass containers and visible alcohol branding are banned, smoking is forbidden on the float, and float drivers and float crew remain completely sober.
  • Everyone obeys law enforcement, parade officials, and Krewe security, full stop. Failure to comply means immediate ejection from the parade and suspension from the next one, and members are 100% responsible for their guests' behavior and attire.

7Bead Etiquette & the Bead-Free Bay📿 Member know-how · throw like a proClick to learn ▾☘

What you'll learn

  • All beads are tossed gently and underhand. Overhand throws are strictly forbidden: aim low and never toward anyone's face.
  • No early throws: beads don't fly until the parade officially reaches its starting point (Bay to Bay Boulevard for Gasparilla).
  • De-bag before you stage: no plastic bags are allowed on the parade route, so all beads come out of their packaging before the parade begins.
  • Hand beads directly to small children near the curb rather than throwing, stay out of arm's reach of the crowd line, and never hand beads to police officers; they are working and need their full attention.
  • Throwing beads into the bay is illegal, and the Krewe proudly supports the #BeadFreeBay conservation campaign with the City of Tampa and The Florida Aquarium, keeping our celebration out of our waterways.

Member Know-How

The Creed of the Krewe

Our Code of Conduct, and why it matters
☘ Click to read the story ▾

A krewe built on friendship needs a few clear rules to protect it. Our Code of Conduct is the quiet backbone of everything we do in public: it keeps the fun safe, the friendships strong, and the krewe's good name untarnished in front of the whole bay. Verbal intimidation, illegal substances, and behavior that puts others at risk have no place on our route or at our tables.

The standard comes with real teeth. A first infraction can bring a reprimand, a fine, or suspension from a parade. A second brings automatic suspension from at least one parade plus a fine. A third means revocation of membership. Excessive drunkenness is never tolerated, and anyone who crosses the line on parade day can be placed on the float for the remainder of the route at the discretion of a board member, the sergeant at arms, or security.

None of this exists to spoil the craic; it exists so there can be craic at all, season after season. When the public sees a krewe that celebrates hard and carries itself well, the whole heritage we represent stands a little taller. That's the Emerald Bond: discipline and friendship, woven together like the cloth we wear.

💚 Krewe Life & Giving Back

Krewe Life

The Morning the Pipes Start

Parade day
☘ Click to read the story ▾

There's a particular kind of magic to parade morning. The kilts come out of their garment bags, the beads get untangled, and somebody's always missing a sporran. Then the pipes start to tune, that first wheezy drone fills the air, and a hundred neighbors suddenly become one krewe.

Watch a first-year member on that morning. They arrived early and a little nervous, fussing with an unfamiliar kilt, wondering if they'd fit in. Three hours later they're waving to strangers like old friends, tossing beads to kids riding high on shoulders, and walking taller than they did at dawn. Nobody teaches that. The route does.

That's the secret we relearn every season: the parade we give Tampa Bay gives back double. Strangers on the curb wave their little flags, children dash for beads, and for a few green-and-gold hours the whole bay feels like family. We march to bring the joy, and we come home carrying more of it than we brought.

Krewe Life

The Family You Choose

Our welcome
☘ Click to read the story ▾

Ask almost anyone in the Krewe what kept them, and they won't say the parades or the party. They'll say the people. Folks arrive knowing one member, or none, and within a season they've got a whole table of friends saving them a seat. Irish or not, kilted or not, everyone's welcome.

Then something quietly wonderful happens. The shy one ends up running a committee. The one who swore they'd never sing in public is the one starting the songs. The couple who joined "just for the parade" is hosting the planning dinner. Nobody sets out to become more here; there's simply room to try, and a whole krewe cheering the moment you do.

"All for Fun, Fun for All" isn't just our motto; it's how a stranger at their first meeting becomes the one tuning the pipes a few years on. New faces, as we like to say, are simply old friends we haven't met yet. Pull up a chair. Yours is next.

Giving Back

More Than a Good Time

Service & charity
☘ Click to read the story ▾

For all the craic, the Krewe has always believed a good time should do some good. Over the years our members have given thousands of dollars and countless hours to Tampa Bay: unloading delivery trucks for families starting over, standing beside children fighting illness, and filling toy drives, food drives, and every gap in between.

It's quiet work, mostly, far from the parade route. No beads, no crowds. Just a Saturday morning, a borrowed truck, and a family stepping into a furnished home for the first time. Ask the members who were there which day of the season they'd never trade, and that's the one they name. It works in a way that's hard to explain and easy to feel: show up to carry someone else's load, and you walk out lighter.

Every hour given is a thread, and the threads become the same strong weave that holds a krewe, and a community, together. If that sounds like your kind of morning, our Service & Charity page is the place to start. Bring a friend. Come see what a good time can build.

Music & Dance

Pipes, Drums & the Music Session

Irish & Scottish tradition
☘ Click to read the story ▾

There is no mistaking the first skirl of the pipes. Whether it's the Great Highland bagpipe of the Scottish tradition or the gentler Irish uilleann pipes, bellows-blown and played sitting down, the sound carries centuries in it. Add the heartbeat of the bodhrán, the goatskin frame drum struck with a little wooden tipper, and you have the engine of any good krewe.

Gather a few players in a corner of a pub and you have a "session": tunes traded by ear, jigs and reels rolling one into the next, no sheet music in sight. And when the fiddle strikes up, the céilí dancing begins: lines and circles and the whirl of a good time shared. It's participation, not performance, and that's exactly the spirit we march in.

Music & Dance

Dancing Irish Women

Steps through time
☘ Click to read the story ▾

From the curled hair and embroidered dresses to the fancy footwork, women's Irish dance carries centuries of heritage in every step. The costumes themselves tell the story: born of the late-19th century Celtic Revival, when a full-length léine, woven crios belt, and flowing brat cape became a proud emblem of Irish identity, and Celtic knotwork from the Book of Kells first found its way onto the dancers' dresses.

Over the decades those costumes have evolved from school uniforms into dazzling, crystal-covered solo dresses, a tradition kept alive by generations of women who march, pipe, and dance right alongside the men. The Smithsonian's Center for Folklife & Cultural Heritage tells the full story beautifully.

12The Gaelic Revival & the Saffron Kilt📜 Heritage · why we wear what we wearClick to learn ▾☘

What you'll learn

  • In the late 1800s and early 1900s, the Gaelic Revival swept Ireland: a movement to reclaim the Irish language, sport, music, and dress as a proud national identity distinct from Britain.
  • The Irish kilt was born in that era. Rather than adopt Scottish clan tartans, revivalists chose solid saffron, a warm golden-orange, and sometimes solid green, as a distinctly Irish national dress.
  • Patrick Pearse adopted the saffron kilt for his Irish-language school, St. Enda's, founded in 1908, and Irish pipe bands took it up soon after; Irish military pipers wear saffron to this day.
  • Irish county tartans (Cork, Kerry, Galway, Dublin, and the rest) are a later creation, designed in the second half of the twentieth century. They hold no official government standing, and that doesn't make them one bit less meaningful: they are a modern, heartfelt way to wear the county your family came from.
  • One more Irish distinction: unlike the back-pleated Scottish standard, Irish kilts in our krewe may feature side pleating, a subtle signature of the Irish silhouette.

13The Irish in America📜 Heritage · the diaspora's storyClick to learn ▾☘

What you'll learn

  • The Great Famine of 1845–1852 changed Ireland forever: roughly a million people died and more than a million emigrated, many crossing the Atlantic to build new lives in America.
  • Irish immigrants and their descendants shaped American cities, labor, music, policing, politics, and celebration, and they built the St. Patrick's Day parade tradition here decades before Ireland held parades of its own.
  • Today, tens of millions of Americans claim Irish ancestry, several times the population of Ireland itself. The diaspora keeps the culture alive far beyond the island's shores.
  • That is exactly where a krewe like ours fits in: heritage is not merely remembered, it is performed. Wearing the kilt, playing the tunes, marching the route, and giving back to the community are how the tradition stays a living thing.
  • And in the spirit of "strength in numbers": you don't need a drop of Irish blood to belong here. Loving the culture and living its generosity is what makes you one of us.

Heritage

High Crosses & the Fields of Clover

Irish heritage
☘ Click to read the story ▾

The great stone high crosses of Ireland, ringed where the cross-arms meet, once stood as gathering points and teaching tools, their panels telling stories to all who passed. Set among the clover, they marry the sacred and the everyday in a way that feels deeply Irish.

And the clover itself? Saint Patrick, so the tradition goes, plucked a humble shamrock to explain the Trinity with its three leaves on a single stem, and the little plant has been ours ever since.

Festivals

The Wheel of the Year

Seasons & celebrations
☘ Click to read the story ▾

The Celtic calendar turns on four great fire-festivals. Imbolc in February greets the first stirrings of spring and Saint Brigid. Bealtaine in May lights the summer fires. Lúnasa in August gives thanks for the harvest. And Samhain at the end of October, when the veil between worlds grows thin, is the ancient root of our modern Halloween.

Of course, the day we hold dearest is the 17th of March. Saint Patrick's Day began as a quiet feast and grew into the world's great celebration of all things Irish, and in Tampa Bay there's no finer place to wear your green than marching with the Krewe.

Legends & Myth

The Salmon of Knowledge

A tale of Éireann
☘ Click to read the story ▾

There is a fish that swam in the Well of Segais, at the source of the River Boyne, and whoever tasted its flesh would gain all the wisdom in the world. The salmon had fed on hazelnuts fallen from the nine sacred trees that ringed the well, and in each nut was the knowledge of the ages.

The poet Finnegas spent seven long years fishing that river, waiting for his chance. When at last he caught the salmon, he set his young apprentice, a boy named Fionn mac Cumhaill, to cook it, but warned him firmly: do not taste a single bite. As Fionn turned the fish over the fire, a blister of hot skin burst and burned his thumb. Without thinking, he pressed it to his mouth to ease the sting, and in that instant, all the wisdom of the world passed into him.

From that day forward, whenever Fionn needed guidance, he had only to press his thumb to his teeth and the knowledge would come. The great hero who would go on to lead the Fianna, Ireland's legendary warrior band, was made not by a sword stroke, but by an accidental taste of something wonderful.

Legends

Daughters & Warriors of Éireann

Irish legend
☘ Click to read the story ▾

Long before the parades, the women of the Celtic world were warriors, queens, and keepers of the law. Queen Medb of Connacht led armies into the great cattle raid of the Táin; the war-goddess The Morrígan wheeled over the battlefield as a crow; and on the Isle of Skye, the warrior-woman Scáthach trained the greatest heroes of the age.

It's a heritage worth remembering: in the Krewe of Shamrock, the women march, pipe, and lead right alongside the men: daughters of Éireann, every one.

Gaelic & Blessings

Sláinte! A Few Words of Irish

As Gaeilge, in the Irish language
☘ Click to read the story ▾

A few words of Irish go a long way. Raise a glass and you'll hear Sláinte (SLAWN-cha), which means "health!" Our own welcome, Céad Míle Fáilte (kayd MEE-luh felt-cha), means "a hundred thousand welcomes," and we mean every one of them.

The old blessings are the loveliest of all: "May the road rise up to meet you, may the wind be always at your back." And when someone sneezes, you might hear Dia linn, "God be with us." You needn't be fluent; a single warm word, well meant, is pure Irish.

Celtic Symbols

The Triquetra: Knot of Three

Irish heritage
☘ Click to read the story ▾

The triquetra, or trinity knot, is among the oldest and best-loved of Celtic symbols. Drawn as a single unbroken line, it has no beginning and no end, a fitting emblem for eternity, unity, and the bonds of community that the early Irish prized above all.

You'll find it carved on standing stones, illuminated in the Book of Kells, and worked into jewelry to this day. We carry it as our quiet reminder that a krewe, like the knot, is stronger for being woven together.
